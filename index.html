Every tsunami on record

1,699 tsunamis from NOAA's Global Historical Tsunami Database, from the eruption of Thera to 2026: what set them off, the 33,176 places where the water was seen, how high it ran, how far it crossed the ocean, and the people it killed.

A tsunami is the sea moving all at once. Something shoves a huge volume of water, usually the sea floor jerking up in an earthquake, sometimes a volcano or a mountainside falling into a bay, and the push spreads out as waves that can cross an ocean in a day. Near the source they can be tens of metres high. Far away they may be a few centimetres on a tide gauge, or a surge that still drowns a harbour.

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) keep a list of every one they can find, from geology and ancient texts to deep-ocean pressure sensors. I took the whole database, 3,138 possible tsunami sources and 35,129 records of where the water arrived, and kept the 1,699 that NCEI rates as definite (1,110) or probable (589). Press play on the map to watch the record build up, or take the tour of the famous waves. Click any source to draw lines to every place its water reached.

    02 · What starts themEarthquakes, mostly, and then mountains falling into the sea

    Of the 1,699 tsunamis, 1,224 (72%) were started by an earthquake alone, and 115 (6.8%) by an earthquake that also set off a landslide, so 79% involve an earthquake. These are mostly big ones: the median magnitude among the 1,219 with one recorded is 7.1, and 7 reached magnitude 9 or more. They cluster on the edges of the tectonic plates: 63% of earthquake sources lie within 100 km of a plate boundary in Peter Bird's model, and 92% within 250 km.

    The rest are stranger. Landslides account for 131 (7.7%), many of them rock falls into fjords and lakes. Volcanoes account for 103 (6.1%), from the collapse of Thera in the Bronze Age to Krakatau in 1883 and Hunga Tonga in 2022. 76 are meteotsunamis, waves pushed by fast-moving storms and pressure jumps rather than by the ground. Two were explosions: the Halifax munitions ship in 1917 and the blasting of Ripple Rock, a reef in a Canadian channel, in 1958. Volcanoes cause few tsunamis but deadly ones: 10% of all recorded tsunami deaths, mostly Krakatau and the collapse of Mount Unzen's flank into Shimabara Bay in 1792.

    03 · WhereTwo in three around the Pacific

    The Pacific is ringed by subduction zones, where one plate dives under another and stores up the strain that great earthquakes release. 1,142 tsunamis (67%) started in NCEI's Pacific regions, and 1,131 (67%) on the Pacific rim itself, the Ring of Fire, leaving out Hawaii. Japan alone accounts for 304 (18%). The Mediterranean, with its long written history, has 232 (14%); the Atlantic and the Caribbean together 197 (12%); the Indian Ocean 98 (5.8%).

    Where tsunamis start, and where people died

    Share of tsunamis (left) and of recorded tsunami deaths (right) by ocean, from NCEI's region of the source. Hover a bar.

    Deaths do not follow the count. The Indian Ocean has only 5.8% of the tsunamis but 50% of the recorded deaths, almost all from a single morning in 2004. The Pacific, with two thirds of the tsunamis, has 38%.

    04 · How high524 metres up a mountainside

    NCEI records a water height for 29,343 places. Most are modest: the median is 2.1 m, and 36% are under a metre. But the record heights are not from the great ocean tsunamis at all. They come from landslides into narrow bays. On 9 July 1958 (10 July in universal time) a magnitude 7.8 earthquake shook a mass of rock into Lituya Bay in Alaska, and the water ran 524.3 m up the opposite slope, stripping the forest. In 2025 a rockslide into Tracy Arm, another Alaskan fjord, sent water 481 m up its walls. All 9 tsunamis with a runup of 100 m or more were set off by a landslide, a volcano, or both.

    For earthquakes alone, the highest is 88 m, at Unga Island, AK after a quake in the Shumagin Islands of Alaska in 1788. In 2011, the Tohoku earthquake drove the water 39.3 m up the coast of Iwate; in 2004 it reached 50.9 m in Indonesia.

    05 · How farAcross whole oceans, at the speed of a jet

    A big tsunami in deep water travels like a shallow wave over the whole ocean, at a speed set by the depth: about 710 km an hour over four kilometres of water. The records agree. For the 2,403 places more than 1,000 km from the source with a recorded travel time, the median speed works out at 680 km an hour (half lie between 600 and 730).

    So the waves that crossed oceans came on a timetable. In 1946 an earthquake and landslide off Unimak Island in the Aleutians destroyed a lighthouse there and, 4.9 hours later and 3,810 km away, struck Hilo in Hawaii, where NCEI records 96 deaths. In 1960 the largest earthquake ever measured, magnitude 9.5 in southern Chile, sent water to Hilo after 14.8 hours (61 deaths there) and to Japan after 22.4 hours, about 17,050 km from Chile. In 2004 the Sumatra wave reached Kenya after 8.9 hours, Tanzania after 9.7 and South Africa after 11.1; Somalia, about 5,040 km away, saw water up to 9.5 m.

    The farthest the water was measured

    For each tsunami, the farthest place where a height above zero was recorded (sea waves only; see below for the eruptions' air waves). The top 10. Click a row to see it on the map.

    The farthest measurement belongs to 2004: 0.2 m on a tide gauge at Baltra Island in the Galápagos, 19,266 km from Sumatra, 29 hours and 7 minutes after the earthquake, having crossed the Indian Ocean and much of the Pacific. Chilean earthquakes fill most of the rest of the list, because the Pacific carries their waves across to China, Taiwan and Japan, nearly half the world away. 75 tsunamis were measured more than 10,000 km from where they began.

    Waves pushed by the air

    Two eruptions moved the sea in a different way. NCEI marks 727 places where the water rose as the pressure wave from an eruption passed through the air: 643 for Hunga Tonga in 2022 and 84 for Krakatau in 1883. Because the air wave circled the globe, so did the disturbance: Hunga Tonga's water was recorded in 67 countries, the most of any event, as far as Tema in Ghana, 18,280 km away, and Krakatau's as far as Colon in Panama. I keep these places on the map but leave them out of the distance records above.

    06 · The dead545,939 deaths in the record

    NCEI records a tsunami death toll for 244 of the 1,699 tsunamis (14%), and together they add up to 545,939 people. That is a floor, not a total: for 73 more NCEI gives only a rough order of magnitude, and for most old events nobody counted. The toll is dominated by a few days. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004 killed 227,899 people, 42% of every tsunami death in the database. The ten deadliest account for 78%.

    The deadliest tsunamis

    Deaths caused by the tsunami, as NCEI records them, for the 12 deadliest. Old figures are estimates. Hover a bar for the event's total, including the earthquake; click to see it on the map.

    After 2004 come Lisbon in 1755 (50,000 tsunami deaths, from an earthquake that also shook the city down and set it on fire), Krakatau in 1883 (34,417), the Meiji Sanriku tsunami in Japan in 1896 (27,122), northern Chile in 1868 (25,000) and Tohoku in 2011 (18,423). Each figure is a town or a coastline emptied. Since 2000 alone, NCEI records 252,964 tsunami deaths.

    NCEI also gives a second figure, the total deaths for the whole event, and it is easy to confuse the two. By that measure the deadliest entry is Haiti in 2010, with 316,000 deaths, but the tsunami that followed the earthquake killed 7. The Great Kanto earthquake of 1923 killed 105,385 people; its tsunami, 325. Everything on this page uses the tsunami's own toll.

    07 · Through timeA record that grows with the people keeping it

    The database starts with the eruption of Thera, dated to 1610 BC, but the early record is thin: only 4 tsunamis before AD 1 and 74 before 1500. Then it fills: 63 in the 1600s, 88 in the 1700s, 282 in the 1800s and 718 in the 1900s. 69% of the record is from 1900 or later.

    Tsunamis recorded per century

    Definite and probable tsunamis by century and cause, AD 1 to 2026 (the 4 earlier ones are left off). The 2000s are partial. Hover a bar.

    This is not a rise in tsunamis. It is a rise in seeing them: writing spreading around the coasts, newspapers, then tide gauges in the 1800s, seismometers in the 1900s and deep-ocean pressure sensors since the 1990s, which catch waves of a few centimetres that nobody on shore would notice. Meteotsunamis show it clearly: 9 before 1950, 57 since 2000. Even in the instrument era the count creeps up, from 63 in 1900 to 1909 to 189 in 2010 to 2019. Since 1950 the database has averaged 11.5 tsunamis a year, and 1.4 a year that killed someone.

    08 · Who is hitJapan, above all

    Counting the places where water was recorded, 140 countries and territories have been reached by at least one tsunami. Japan has been reached by the most: 395 different tsunamis, followed by the United States (264) and Indonesia (138). Japan also has the most measurements, 12,724, 38% of all places in the database, 6,326 of them from the survey after Tohoku in 2011.

    09 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. NOAA NCEI's Global Historical Tsunami Database, fetched from the NCEI Hazard Service API in 2026: 3,138 source events and 35,129 runup records. NCEI rates each source from 4 (definite) to 3 (probable), 2 (questionable), 1 (very doubtful), 0 (a seiche or disturbance in an inland river or lake only) and minus 1 (an erroneous entry). I count only 3 and 4: 1,699 tsunamis, leaving out 1,439 entries. The cause codes are NCEI's: 1 earthquake, 2 questionable earthquake, 3 earthquake and landslide, 4 volcano and earthquake, 5 volcano, earthquake and landslide, 6 volcano, 7 volcano and landslide, 8 landslide, 9 meteorological, 10 explosion, 11 astronomical tide, 0 unknown. I checked them against events whose cause is not in doubt (the Halifax explosion is 10, the Vajont dam landslide 8, Krakatau 6) and grouped them as shown.

    Places. A runup record is a place where NCEI records the water: a survey mark, a tide gauge or an eyewitness account. I keep the 33,532 records that belong to a definite or probable tsunami and are not flagged as doubtful (1,248 are). NCEI also flags places where the water was moved by an eruption's air wave or by the weather; I keep these, and leave the air wave places out of the distance records. 356 records come from deep-ocean pressure gauges far out at sea; they are left off the map and out of the country counts. Distance from the source and travel time are NCEI's. Oceans come from NCEI's region of the source; the Ring of Fire here means the regions around the rim of the Pacific (Japan, Kamchatka, Alaska, the Americas' Pacific coasts, the western Pacific islands, eastern Indonesia, New Zealand), not Hawaii; it leaves out the 63 tsunamis off Sumatra and Java, which NCEI places in the Indian Ocean.

    Deaths. I use NCEI's count of deaths caused by the tsunami, never the total for the whole event, and I do not add up deaths over runup places, because one town can appear several times. Many historical figures are estimates, and some are disputed. The record before 1900 is very incomplete, and the counts per century mostly measure how much was written down.

    The oldest definite tsunami in the database: the eruption and collapse of Thera (Santorini) in the Aegean, dated to 1610 BC. NCEI gives a maximum water height of 90 m and two places, the north and east coasts of Crete and Ugarit on the Syrian coast, but no death toll. For events this old the date and the size are reconstructions from geology and archaeology.
    On the morning of 1 November 1755 a magnitude 8.5 earthquake off Portugal shook Lisbon, and the sea followed. NCEI records 50,000 tsunami deaths and water up to 18.3 m at Cadiz in Spain. It was seen in 24 countries, as far as Samana Bay in the Dominican Republic, 6,050 km away.
    In January 1700 the Cascadia fault off the Pacific coast of North America broke in a magnitude 9.0 earthquake. Nobody wrote it down there. Every one of the 7 places in the database is in Japan, where a tsunami arrived with no earthquake felt, up to 5.4 m at Miho, about 7,580 km from the source.
    On 27 August 1883 the volcano of Krakatau, between Java and Sumatra, blew itself apart. The waves killed 34,417 people around the Sunda Strait, NCEI records, and reached 41.0 m at Merak, Java. The eruption's air wave then moved the sea around the world: 84 places in 18 countries, as far as Colon, Panama.
    On 15 June 1896 a magnitude 8.3 earthquake off the Sanriku coast of north-east Japan sent water up to 38.2 m at Shirahama. NCEI records 27,122 deaths, the fourth highest toll in the database. The wave was measured as far as Rarotonga in the Cook Islands, 8,910 km away.
    On 1 April 1946 a magnitude 8.6 earthquake and landslide off Unimak Island in the Aleutians sent water 42.2 m up the cliff at Scotch Cap, Unimak Is, AK, where it destroyed the lighthouse. Less than five hours later (4.9) it reached Hilo in Hawaii, 3,810 km away, where NCEI records 96 deaths. In all, 168 people died.
    On the evening of 9 July 1958 (10 July in universal time, the date NCEI uses) a magnitude 7.8 earthquake knocked a mass of rock into the head of Lituya Bay in Alaska. The water ran 524.3 m up the slope opposite, the highest runup ever recorded, and swept the bay. NCEI records 5 deaths. Choose Height as the size to see how far above every other source it sits.
    On 22 May 1960 southern Chile had the largest earthquake ever measured, magnitude 9.5. The tsunami killed 2,226 people, NCEI records, and crossed the Pacific: Hilo after 14.8 hours, Japan after 22.4. It was measured in 35 countries, as far as Hong Kong, 18,100 km away.
    On 28 March 1964 (27 March local time) a magnitude 9.2 earthquake struck Prince William Sound in Alaska, with underwater landslides in its fjords. The water reached 67.1 m at Cliff Mine, Valdez Inlet. NCEI records 124 tsunami deaths, and the wave was measured in 27 countries, as far as Antarctica.
    On 26 December 2004 a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra caused the deadliest tsunami on record: 227,899 people, NCEI records, around the whole Indian Ocean. Water reached 50.9 m in Indonesia. It was measured in 48 countries, the most of any sea wave, and as far as Baltra Island in the Galápagos, 19,270 km away.
    On 11 March 2011 a magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Tohoku in Japan sent water 39.3 m up the coast of Iwate. NCEI records 18,423 deaths. Japan's surveys after the disaster produced 6,326 measurements, more than any other event. The wave reached Chile after 21.3 hours.
    On 15 January 2022 the undersea volcano Hunga Tonga Hunga Ha'apai erupted. Water reached 22.0 m in Tonga and NCEI records 6 deaths. Its air wave circled the planet and moved the sea in 67 countries, the most of any event, as far as Tema in Ghana.
