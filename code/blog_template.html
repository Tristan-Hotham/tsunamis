I mapped every tsunami in NOAA's Global Historical Tsunami Database, {{source.n|int}} of them since the eruption of Thera, and every place their water was recorded: where they start, how high the sea ran, how far it travelled and who it killed.

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information keep a list of every tsunami they can find evidence for, from Bronze Age geology to the deep-ocean pressure sensors that now catch waves a few centimetres high. The list is free for anyone to use. It holds {{source.events|int}} possible sources, of which NCEI rates {{source.n|int}} as definite or probable tsunamis, and {{source.runups|int}} records of where the water arrived: survey marks, tide gauges and eyewitness reports. I put them all on one map.

Earthquakes, mostly, and mostly in the Pacific

The pattern is unmistakable. {{causes.Earthquake.pct|int}}% of tsunamis were started by an earthquake alone, and {{eq_any_pct|int}}% involved one. They trace the edges of the tectonic plates: {{plates.within100_pct|int}}% of earthquake sources lie within 100 km of a plate boundary. {{ring.pct|int}}% began on the rim of the Pacific, the Ring of Fire, and Japan alone accounts for {{ring.japan_pct|int}}%. The rest are a gallery of oddities: {{causes.Landslide.n|int}} landslides, {{causes.Volcano.n|int}} volcanic events, {{causes.Meteorological.n|int}} meteotsunamis pushed by storms, and two explosions, including the Halifax munitions ship of 1917.

{{IMG|b_causes.png|Four bar charts. By cause, earthquakes are 72% of tsunamis and 85% of deaths; volcanoes are 6% of tsunamis but 10% of deaths; landslides 8% of tsunamis but under 1% of deaths. By ocean, the Pacific has 67% of tsunamis but 38% of deaths, while the Indian Ocean has 6% of tsunamis and 50% of deaths|Share of tsunamis and of recorded tsunami deaths, by cause and by ocean.}}

Deaths follow a different map. The Indian Ocean has only {{oceans.Indian Ocean.pct|f1}}% of the tsunamis but {{oceans.Indian Ocean.deaths_pct|int}}% of the recorded deaths, almost all from a single morning.

The dead

NCEI records a death toll for {{deaths.n_events|int}} tsunamis, adding up to {{deaths.total|int}} people, and that is a floor: for most old events nobody counted. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004 killed {{deadliest.0.deaths|int}}, {{deaths.y2004_pct|int}}% of every tsunami death in the database. Then come Lisbon in 1755 ({{deadliest.1.deaths|int}}), Krakatau in 1883 ({{deadliest.2.deaths|int}}), the Meiji Sanriku tsunami in Japan in 1896 ({{deadliest.3.deaths|int}}), northern Chile in 1868 ({{deadliest.4.deaths|int}}) and Tohoku in 2011 ({{deadliest.5.deaths|int}}).

{{IMG|b_deadliest.png|Bar chart of the twelve deadliest tsunamis. Off Sumatra 2004 towers over the rest at 227,899 deaths, followed by Lisbon 1755 at 50,000, Krakatau 1883 at 34,417, Sanriku 1896 at 27,122, northern Chile 1868 at 25,000 and Honshu 2011 at 18,423|The twelve deadliest tsunamis, by deaths caused by the tsunami as NCEI records them.}}

One trap in the data is worth knowing about. NCEI also gives a total for the whole event, and by that measure its deadliest entry is Haiti in 2010, with {{haiti.deaths_total|int}} deaths. The tsunami after that earthquake killed {{haiti.deaths|int}}. I use the tsunami's own toll throughout.

{{famous.lituya.max_run|int}} metres up a mountain

The highest water on record has nothing to do with the great ocean tsunamis. In July 1958 an earthquake knocked a mass of rock into Lituya Bay in Alaska, and the water ran {{famous.lituya.max_run|f1}} m up the slope opposite, stripping it of forest. Every tsunami that ran 100 m or more involved a landslide or a volcano: Tracy Arm in Alaska in 2025 ({{highest.1.ht|int}} m), Spirit Lake at Mount St Helens in 1980, the Vajont dam in Italy in 1963, Dickson Fjord in Greenland in 2023. For an earthquake alone, the record is {{heights.eq_max.ht|int}} m.

{{IMG|b_highest.png|Bar chart of the twelve highest runups: Lituya Bay 1958 at 524 m, Tracy Arm 2025 at 481 m, Spirit Lake 1980 at 250 m, Casso (Vajont) 1963 at 235 m, Dickson Fjord 2023 at 200 m, then Taan Fjord, Lituya Bay twice more and others down to 90 m, almost all purple for landslides|The highest runup recorded for each tsunami, coloured by cause.}}

Across oceans, at the speed of a jet

The great earthquake tsunamis go the other way: not high, but far. Over deep ocean a tsunami moves at roughly the speed of an airliner, and the records show it. For places more than 1,000 km from the source, the median speed works out at {{speed.median_kmh|int}} km an hour. The 1960 Chilean tsunami reached Hilo in Hawaii after {{travel.chile_hilo.first_h|f1}} hours and Japan, on the far side of the Pacific, after {{travel.chile_japan.first_h|f1}}. The 2004 tsunami reached Kenya after {{travel.sum_kenya.first_h|f1}} hours and South Africa after {{travel.sum_safrica.first_h|f1}}, and was still measurable on a tide gauge at {{farthest.0.place|raw}} in the Galápagos, {{farthest.0.dist|int}} km away, {{farthest.0.trav_h|int}} hours after the earthquake.

More tsunamis, or more people watching?

The record grows steeply with time: {{cent.c1600|int}} tsunamis in the 1600s, {{cent.c1800|int}} in the 1800s, {{cent.c1900|int}} in the 1900s. That is not a world getting more dangerous. It is writing, then newspapers, then tide gauges, seismometers and deep-ocean sensors, each catching smaller waves than the last. Meteotsunamis make the point: {{modern.met_before1950|int}} recorded before 1950, {{modern.met_since2000|int}} since 2000.

How I did this

Everything comes from the NCEI Global Historical Tsunami Database, fetched from NOAA's Hazard Service in {{source.fetched|raw}}. I kept the {{source.n|int}} events NCEI rates as definite or probable, grouped its cause codes, and used its regions for the oceans. Deaths are NCEI's count of people killed by the tsunami, not the total for the earthquake. I dropped runup records flagged as doubtful, kept deep-ocean gauges off the map, and treated the water moved by the air waves of Krakatau and Hunga Tonga separately from sea waves. Plate boundaries are Peter Bird's PB2002 model. The paper sets out every step and caveat.
