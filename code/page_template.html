A tsunami is the sea moving all at once. Something shoves a huge volume of water, usually the sea floor jerking up in an earthquake, sometimes a volcano or a mountainside falling into a bay, and the push spreads out as waves that can cross an ocean in a day. Near the source they can be tens of metres high. Far away they may be a few centimetres on a tide gauge, or a surge that still drowns a harbour.

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) keep a list of every one they can find, from geology and ancient texts to deep-ocean pressure sensors. I took the whole database, {{source.events|int}} possible tsunami sources and {{source.runups|int}} records of where the water arrived, and kept the {{source.n|int}} that NCEI rates as definite ({{source.definite|int}}) or probable ({{source.probable|int}}). Press play on the map to watch the record build up, or take the tour of the famous waves. Click any source to draw lines to every place its water reached.

    02 · What starts themEarthquakes, mostly, and then mountains falling into the sea

    Of the {{source.n|int}} tsunamis, {{causes.Earthquake.n|int}} ({{causes.Earthquake.pct|int}}%) were started by an earthquake alone, and {{causes.Earthquake and landslide.n|int}} ({{causes.Earthquake and landslide.pct|f1}}%) by an earthquake that also set off a landslide, so {{eq_any_pct|int}}% involve an earthquake. These are mostly big ones: the median magnitude among the {{mag.n|int}} with one recorded is {{mag.median|f1}}, and {{mag.ge9|int}} reached magnitude 9 or more. They cluster on the edges of the tectonic plates: {{plates.within100_pct|int}}% of earthquake sources lie within 100 km of a plate boundary in Peter Bird's model, and {{plates.within250_pct|int}}% within 250 km.

    The rest are stranger. Landslides account for {{causes.Landslide.n|int}} ({{causes.Landslide.pct|f1}}%), many of them rock falls into fjords and lakes. Volcanoes account for {{causes.Volcano.n|int}} ({{causes.Volcano.pct|f1}}%), from the collapse of Thera in the Bronze Age to Krakatau in 1883 and Hunga Tonga in 2022. {{causes.Meteorological.n|int}} are meteotsunamis, waves pushed by fast-moving storms and pressure jumps rather than by the ground. Two were explosions: the Halifax munitions ship in 1917 and the blasting of Ripple Rock, a reef in a Canadian channel, in 1958. Volcanoes cause few tsunamis but deadly ones: {{causes.Volcano.deaths_pct|int}}% of all recorded tsunami deaths, mostly Krakatau and the collapse of Mount Unzen's flank into Shimabara Bay in 1792.

    03 · WhereTwo in three around the Pacific

    The Pacific is ringed by subduction zones, where one plate dives under another and stores up the strain that great earthquakes release. {{oceans.Pacific.n|int}} tsunamis ({{oceans.Pacific.pct|int}}%) started in NCEI's Pacific regions, and {{ring.n|int}} ({{ring.pct|int}}%) on the Pacific rim itself, the Ring of Fire, leaving out Hawaii. Japan alone accounts for {{ring.japan|int}} ({{ring.japan_pct|int}}%). The Mediterranean, with its long written history, has {{oceans.Mediterranean.n|int}} ({{oceans.Mediterranean.pct|int}}%); the Atlantic and the Caribbean together {{oceans.Atlantic and Caribbean.n|int}} ({{oceans.Atlantic and Caribbean.pct|int}}%); the Indian Ocean {{oceans.Indian Ocean.n|int}} ({{oceans.Indian Ocean.pct|f1}}%).

    04 · How high{{famous.lituya.max_run|int}} metres up a mountainside

    NCEI records a water height for {{heights.n_ht|int}} places. Most are modest: the median is {{heights.median|f1}} m, and {{heights.under1_pct|int}}% are under a metre. But the record heights are not from the great ocean tsunamis at all. They come from landslides into narrow bays. On 9 July 1958 (10 July in universal time) a magnitude {{famous.lituya.mag|f1}} earthquake shook a mass of rock into Lituya Bay in Alaska, and the water ran {{famous.lituya.max_run|f1}} m up the opposite slope, stripping the forest. In 2025 a rockslide into Tracy Arm, another Alaskan fjord, sent water {{highest.1.ht|int}} m up its walls. All {{heights.ge100_events|int}} tsunamis with a runup of 100 m or more were set off by a landslide, a volcano, or both.

    05 · How farAcross whole oceans, at the speed of a jet

    A big tsunami in deep water travels like a shallow wave over the whole ocean, at a speed set by the depth: about {{speed.theory_4km_kmh|int}} km an hour over four kilometres of water. The records agree. For the {{speed.n|int}} places more than 1,000 km from the source with a recorded travel time, the median speed works out at {{speed.median_kmh|int}} km an hour (half lie between {{speed.q25|int}} and {{speed.q75|int}}).

    So the waves that crossed oceans came on a timetable. In 1946 an earthquake and landslide off Unimak Island in the Aleutians destroyed a lighthouse there and, {{travel.aleut_hilo.first_h|f1}} hours later and {{travel.aleut_hilo.dist|int}} km away, struck Hilo in Hawaii, where NCEI records {{travel.aleut_hilo.deaths_max|int}} deaths. In 1960 the largest earthquake ever measured, magnitude {{famous.chile.mag|f1}} in southern Chile, sent water to Hilo after {{travel.chile_hilo.first_h|f1}} hours ({{travel.chile_hilo.deaths_max|int}} deaths there) and to Japan after {{travel.chile_japan.first_h|f1}} hours, about {{travel.chile_japan.dist|int}} km from Chile. In 2004 the Sumatra wave reached Kenya after {{travel.sum_kenya.first_h|f1}} hours, Tanzania after {{travel.sum_tanzania.first_h|f1}} and South Africa after {{travel.sum_safrica.first_h|f1}}; Somalia, about {{travel.sum_somalia.dist|int}} km away, saw water up to {{travel.sum_somalia.max_ht|f1}} m.

    The farthest measurement belongs to 2004: {{farthest.0.ht|f1}} m on a tide gauge at {{farthest.0.place|raw}} in the Galápagos, {{farthest.0.dist|int}} km from Sumatra, {{farthest.0.trav_h|int}} hours and {{farthest.0.trav_m|int}} minutes after the earthquake, having crossed the Indian Ocean and much of the Pacific. Chilean earthquakes fill most of the rest of the list, because the Pacific carries their waves across to China, Taiwan and Japan, nearly half the world away. {{dist.over10000_events|int}} tsunamis were measured more than 10,000 km from where they began.

    Waves pushed by the air

    Two eruptions moved the sea in a different way. NCEI marks {{atm.n|int}} places where the water rose as the pressure wave from an eruption passed through the air: {{atm.tonga|int}} for Hunga Tonga in 2022 and {{atm.krakatau|int}} for Krakatau in 1883. Because the air wave circled the globe, so did the disturbance: Hunga Tonga's water was recorded in {{famous.tonga.countries|int}} countries, the most of any event, as far as {{atm.tonga_far_place|raw}} in {{atm.tonga_far_country|raw}}, {{atm.tonga_far_km|int}} km away, and Krakatau's as far as {{atm.krakatau_far_place|raw}} in {{atm.krakatau_far_country|raw}}. I keep these places on the map but leave them out of the distance records above.

    06 · The dead{{deaths.total|int}} deaths in the record

    NCEI records a tsunami death toll for {{deaths.n_events|int}} of the {{source.n|int}} tsunamis ({{deaths.n_events_pct|int}}%), and together they add up to {{deaths.total|int}} people. That is a floor, not a total: for {{deaths.only_order|int}} more NCEI gives only a rough order of magnitude, and for most old events nobody counted. The toll is dominated by a few days. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004 killed {{deadliest.0.deaths|int}} people, {{deaths.y2004_pct|int}}% of every tsunami death in the database. The ten deadliest account for {{deaths.top10_pct|int}}%.

    After 2004 come Lisbon in 1755 ({{deadliest.1.deaths|int}} tsunami deaths, from an earthquake that also shook the city down and set it on fire), Krakatau in 1883 ({{deadliest.2.deaths|int}}), the Meiji Sanriku tsunami in Japan in 1896 ({{deadliest.3.deaths|int}}), northern Chile in 1868 ({{deadliest.4.deaths|int}}) and Tohoku in 2011 ({{deadliest.5.deaths|int}}). Each figure is a town or a coastline emptied. Since 2000 alone, NCEI records {{deaths.since2000|int}} tsunami deaths.

    NCEI also gives a second figure, the total deaths for the whole event, and it is easy to confuse the two. By that measure the deadliest entry is Haiti in 2010, with {{haiti.deaths_total|int}} deaths, but the tsunami that followed the earthquake killed {{haiti.deaths|int}}. The Great Kanto earthquake of 1923 killed {{kanto.deaths_total|int}} people; its tsunami, {{kanto.deaths|int}}. Everything on this page uses the tsunami's own toll.

    07 · Through timeA record that grows with the people keeping it

    The database starts with the eruption of Thera, dated to {{source.first_name|raw}}, but the early record is thin: only {{cent.before0|int}} tsunamis before AD 1 and {{cent.before1500|int}} before 1500. Then it fills: {{cent.c1600|int}} in the 1600s, {{cent.c1700|int}} in the 1700s, {{cent.c1800|int}} in the 1800s and {{cent.c1900|int}} in the 1900s. {{cent.since1900_pct|int}}% of the record is from 1900 or later.

    Tsunamis recorded per century

    Definite and probable tsunamis by century and cause, AD 1 to {{source.last_year|raw}} (the {{cent.before0|int}} earlier ones are left off). The 2000s are partial. Hover a bar.

    This is not a rise in tsunamis. It is a rise in seeing them: writing spreading around the coasts, newspapers, then tide gauges in the 1800s, seismometers in the 1900s and deep-ocean pressure sensors since the 1990s, which catch waves of a few centimetres that nobody on shore would notice. Meteotsunamis show it clearly: {{modern.met_before1950|int}} before 1950, {{modern.met_since2000|int}} since 2000. Even in the instrument era the count creeps up, from {{decades.0.n|int}} in 1900 to 1909 to {{decades.11.n|int}} in 2010 to 2019. Since 1950 the database has averaged {{modern.per_year|f1}} tsunamis a year, and {{modern.deadly_per_year|f1}} a year that killed someone.

    09 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. NOAA NCEI's Global Historical Tsunami Database, fetched from the NCEI Hazard Service API in {{source.fetched|raw}}: {{source.events|int}} source events and {{source.runups|int}} runup records. NCEI rates each source from 4 (definite) to 3 (probable), 2 (questionable), 1 (very doubtful), 0 (a seiche or disturbance in an inland river or lake only) and minus 1 (an erroneous entry). I count only 3 and 4: {{source.n|int}} tsunamis, leaving out {{source.other|int}} entries. The cause codes are NCEI's: 1 earthquake, 2 questionable earthquake, 3 earthquake and landslide, 4 volcano and earthquake, 5 volcano, earthquake and landslide, 6 volcano, 7 volcano and landslide, 8 landslide, 9 meteorological, 10 explosion, 11 astronomical tide, 0 unknown. I checked them against events whose cause is not in doubt (the Halifax explosion is 10, the Vajont dam landslide 8, Krakatau 6) and grouped them as shown.

    Places. A runup record is a place where NCEI records the water: a survey mark, a tide gauge or an eyewitness account. I keep the {{source.runups_kept|int}} records that belong to a definite or probable tsunami and are not flagged as doubtful ({{source.runups_doubtful|int}} are). NCEI also flags places where the water was moved by an eruption's air wave or by the weather; I keep these, and leave the air wave places out of the distance records. {{source.runups_deep|int}} records come from deep-ocean pressure gauges far out at sea; they are left off the map and out of the country counts. Distance from the source and travel time are NCEI's. Oceans come from NCEI's region of the source; the Ring of Fire here means the regions around the rim of the Pacific (Japan, Kamchatka, Alaska, the Americas' Pacific coasts, the western Pacific islands, eastern Indonesia, New Zealand), not Hawaii; it leaves out the {{ring.sunda|int}} tsunamis off Sumatra and Java, which NCEI places in the Indian Ocean.

    Deaths. I use NCEI's count of deaths caused by the tsunami, never the total for the whole event, and I do not add up deaths over runup places, because one town can appear several times. Many historical figures are estimates, and some are disputed. The record before 1900 is very incomplete, and the counts per century mostly measure how much was written down.
